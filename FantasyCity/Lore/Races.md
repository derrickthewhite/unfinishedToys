# Races

This document focuses on each race's body plan, visible appearance, and the social effects of how it reproduces.

## Striders

Striders are human beings with an upright posture, two arms, two legs, grasping hands, flat nails, expressive eyebrows, and highly mobile faces. Their bodies show the full ordinary human range: short to tall, narrow to broad, wiry to heavyset. Skin tones range from very pale pink-beige through freckled tan, olive, bronze, umber, and deep brown. Hair may be black, brown, blond, auburn, red, gray, or white, and can be straight, wavy, curly, or tightly coiled. Facial hair, body hair, eye color, and facial structure vary widely from one population and household to another.

Their social life is shaped by sexual reproduction, pregnancy, and long childhood dependence. Because striders usually know who bore and raised them, they build durable ideas of parenthood, kinship, inheritance, and descent. Households, marriages, extended families, and bloodlines matter far more to them than they do to races whose young are spawned, built, or communally claimed after birth. Even where law or custom is flexible, strider culture tends to treat family as a primary social unit.

## Clankers

Clankers are large, horizontal, six-legged constructed beings made from wood, metal, and glass. Their bodies are built around a central wooden platform that carries their glass energy blocks, brain assemblies, and sensory apparatus. Six spider-like legs support the frame, with the front pair larger than the others, and a smaller pair of clawed forelimbs at the front handle fine manipulation. Brass joints, inlaid metals, polished wood, and exposed glass components make them look more like crafted machines or ritual instruments than flesh-and-blood animals.

Clankers are built rather than born, and their reproductive culture dominates their society. Most new clankers begin as template minds: valuable proto-minds that are not yet persons, though they become persons once housed and developed. Designers alter each new template individually, while Growers use a common template line for an entire clan. A true clanker's identity is tied to the original glass housing that contains the mind. That housing can be moved from body to body freely, and doing so is mundane; the clanker remains the same individual. Archiving is the semi-religious and practical act of removing that original housing intact and storing it in a vault, usually as part of an orderly transfer of body, energy sources, work, and duties to a successor. Restoring an archived clanker means reinstalling that same housing into a body, and it is understood as the return of the old individual, not the creation of a new one.

Cloning begins when a housed mind is copied into another glass housing. In practice that means one mind in one housing becomes two copies in two housings, which is why clankers treat any transfer between housings as cloning rather than movement. That taboo is central to clanker religion and social order. Because lineage depends on makers, templates, workshops, and archival succession rather than sex or gestation, clankers organize themselves around creators, vaults, clans, and schools of design instead of biological families.

## Floaters

Floaters are small, spherical, levitating creatures held aloft by magic. Each has a round body with eyes set near the center, a ring of tentacles above and below, and a pair of thin arms ending in matched claw-hands like tiny lobster pincers. The skin over the main body is leathery and stretched over a firm shell, while the tentacles are softer and more flexible. Their usual colors are dark greys, blacks, and browns with occasional greens, and many paint their bodies in bright decorative patterns.

Floater society is built around an enormous reproductive asymmetry. The intelligent, mobile floaters are all male; the female is a huge, non-sapient, non-motile leathery mass lying on the bottom of a central breeding pond, usually weighing roughly 300 to 500 pounds. A viable colony needs a substantial pond, large enough to support the mother and her young but not so large that it becomes difficult to defend or manage. Because all members of a colony are emotionally bound to the same mother, the breeding pond becomes the physical and symbolic center of settlement. Status, leadership, and conflict all circle around access to and protection of the mother and young. Rival colonies may seize another colony's pond and use control of the mother as leverage, while floaters who live away from the colony usually remain low status because they are cut off from the center of reproduction and succession.

## Swimmers

Swimmers are long, horizontal marine people with eight limbs and a low, streamlined body. They usually move along the sea floor rather than through open water, using four limbs for walking and the front four as manipulators when working. Their heads are short and blunt, with four vertebrate-style eyes set into the skull, and their skin is smooth like a shark's and usually pale in neutral tones. Each manipulative limb ends in three long fingers tipped with a single bony claw.

Swimmers reproduce by spawning in the open ocean. Their young develop far from settled communities and arrive at the coast as juveniles, so they do not organize society around known parents or stable nuclear families. Instead, culture is transmitted by whichever group takes in the young. In apprentice societies, juveniles are absorbed into trades, fishing crews, or work groups that need more members. In institute societies, organized settlements gather juveniles into formal systems of training and produce stronger civic continuity, especially in denser coastal centers. The third model is the band: juveniles left to raise themselves form their own groups, which tend to become feral, technologically weak, and aggressive. As a result, swimmer society is strongly shaped by whether a coast has the institutions to claim its young before those young harden into independent bands.
